**Abstract:** The parable puts on display the beauty of God's character. God's forgiveness is always available. Our need is to turn to God and take what he offers in His grace. Also, we need to accept those who seek forgiveness, for there is joy in heaven over those who repent.

---

Luke 15 has three well-known parables which form a section within Jesus' journey to Jerusalem (9:51-19:44). The three parables are the Lost Sheep (15:4-7), the Lost Coin (15:8-10) and the Prodigal Son (15:11-32). These three parallel stories reveal God's desire to find the lost sinner. The chapter opens with the statement that "the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him" (v. 1). Seeing this "the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, 'This man receives sinners and eats with them'" (v. 2). Jesus responds to this criticism with a multifaceted portrait explained in three parables.

The first two parables are similar. In both cases the basic theme is the search, and the basic emotion expressed is joy. They illustrate Jesus' explanation of why he relates to tax collectors and sinners. God is willing to take great efforts and celebrate with great joy to find and restore a sinner to Himself. The point of Jesus' teaching is that God is a God who searches, finds, and cares for the sinner. In a culture where tax collectors were loathed and sinners were scorned, Jesus gives a word that reassures the outcasts to come to Him. God's arms are wide open to receive anyone who comes to Him on His terms.

The third parable in Luke 15 is popularly known as "the Prodigal son." It is intended to demonstrate heaven's receptiveness toward a sinner's repentance, as well as to reprimand the protest of those who react against such divine generosity. The Pharisees complained that Jesus eats with sinners. Jesus, in turn, in the parable, speaks of a father who is willing to extend table fellowship to his repentant son. In other words, the parable boldly presents a God who is pleased to have the penitent at His table.

"The Prodigal son" begins with a quick introduction that simply lists the three characters – a father and two sons (v. 11). The two sons control the story's action, but it is the father's response to the sons that supplies the parable's lessons. The focus of the story is on the father.

The story swiftly moves to a crucial scene (v. 12). The younger son comes to the father with a request to acquire the property that will eventually be his. The request's background has been a subject of much deliberation, since estates usually were not divided until the father's death. Whatever the case maybe, one thing is certain – the son clearly looks to sever his relationship with his father and walk away. What's surprising, however, is that the son's request is graciously granted.

The younger son takes his share of the property and after some time departs for a distant land (v. 13). His life falls apart soon after he leaves his father. In the name of freedom, he squanders all his inheritance in reckless and wild living. His approach to life brings about his ruin. He has no money left. Even nature doesn't seem to help – there is a severe famine. Imagine his predicament – no money, no family, on the brink of poverty, and nowhere to turn!

The young man responds to the situation sensibly. Desperate for food and money, he looks for employment. He hires himself out to a Gentile, as the nature of his work shows, and is sent to feed pigs (v. 15). This was the most shameful work for a Jew, since pigs were unclean animals. Essentially, the younger son has taken the lowest job possible – one that no Jew would even imagine to do. Yet, the job is unable to meet his needs. He is still hungry. In fact, the pigs are better off than he is. He strongly desires to eat what unclean animals eat. How much lower can he go?

The young man's condition finally makes him realize how far he has plunged. Here's the irony in his situation – his father's hired servants have more than enough to eat. In contrast, the son is starving (v. 17). So, he comes up with a plan of action. Here's a key turning point in the story. The beleaguered son decides to confess his folly before God and to his father. He resolves to act quickly and humbly. He is fully aware that he has lost all rights to sonship and inheritance, but it is better to throw himself onto his father's mercy than remain in a distant city, living a despicable life and suffering hunger. He decides to come to his father bringing nothing but his need. There are no excuses, only confession and a humble request. The son's actions portray what repentance looks like – no claims, just dependence on God's mercy and provision.

The young man starts his journey back not knowing what to expect. The father recognizes him while he is still far away and reacts at once with compassion. Breaking all etiquette, the father runs to his son, gives him a big hug, and kisses him. Accordingly, the father now becomes the focus of the parable. The son hasn't said a word, but he has been accepted (v. 20). A relationship has been restored. With repentance comes reconciliation. All of these details powerfully portray the love of God.

The father accepts the son back with full privileges – best clothes, a ring, and shoes. The son moves from destitution to restoration. The picture of joy continues – the father proclaims a celebration in honor of his son's return. What is the reason for this celebration? In the father's own words, "This my son was dead, and is alive again; he was lost, and is found" (v. 24). Repentance brings new life and recovery of the lost!

The focus now shifts to the elder brother's response to the father's generosity. A dutiful son, he had been working in the fields and so was not in the know about the celebration (v. 25). One of the servants explains the reason for the festivities – the younger brother has returned safe and sound. The elder brother is not pleased with the party. Actually, he is angry! He had been faithful to his father all along. Is this a display of favoritism on his father's part? The elder son stays outside the house and refuses to go in. The apparent insider is an outsider.

The father again takes the initiative, coming out and pleading with the son to take part in the celebration (v. 28). Reconciliation with the father should extend to all members of the family. The elder son is cold to his brother's safe return. He calls for justice, making comparisons with his father's treatment of him. The father's joy contrasts with the elder brother's anger. It is all a matter of perspective.

The elder brother's concern for fairness is understandable. But the point of the parable is that God's action is gracious, not deserved. Repentance elicits God's favor, which wipes the slate clean. This is a reason to rejoice. The elder brother is so preoccupied with the issue of fairness that he cannot bring himself to rejoice at the positive transformation that has come to his brother. How can his father be so irresponsible and throw a party for such a disreputable person? In fact, the brother is complaining that infamy holds more value in the eyes of the father than faithfulness. Where is justice? (v. 30).

The father has a pleasant response. He immediately affirms the faithfulness of his elder son and accepts that this son has always been at his side. He reminds the son that all he owns belongs to him and nothing in any way diminishes the son's status. The elder son should not fail to remember the benefits he has always had because of his proximity to the father. In other words, he has always had access to the celebration! (v. 31).

The father, however, will not allow the elder son to persist in his complaints, nor will he allow him to distance himself from his brother. So, the father speaks of the younger brother as "this your brother" (v. 32). Put differently, "He is not just my son – he is your brother!" The father upholds the necessity of celebration. It was appropriate to rejoice. A resurrection of sorts has taken place. A dead brother is now alive. That which was lost has been found. Such events should result in joy, not questions about fairness.

Jesus teaches two key truths in this parable. First, repentance produces a total reversal. Not only is the repentant sinner restored, but also welcomed by the heavenly Father with joy and full acceptance. The Father receives such a person with open arms and offers his home. Great celebration ensues when a sinner comes to the Father. Second, there is an exhortation to receive the repentant one with joy. This reflects God's response!
